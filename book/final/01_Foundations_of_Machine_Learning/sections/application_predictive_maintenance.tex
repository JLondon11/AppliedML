\subsection{Application: Predictive Maintenance for Industrial Rotating Machinery}

Predictive maintenance is a useful Foundations application because it forces the full learning problem to be specified before a classifier is trained. Vibration recordings are not naturally tabular examples: they must first be segmented into windows, transformed into features, and separated into training and evaluation sets in a way that prevents nearly identical samples from the same physical recording from leaking across the split. The resulting problem therefore connects representation, validation, class definition, and metric choice in a single engineering workflow.

Bearing-fault diagnosis also illustrates why dataset provenance matters. The Case Western Reserve University (CWRU) Bearing Data Center provides controlled recordings of normal and seeded bearing-fault conditions at several motor loads. The normal baseline files and the 12-kHz drive-end fault files are not interchangeable without preprocessing: the normal recordings are sampled at a higher rate and must be aligned before time- or frequency-domain features are compared. Individual MATLAB files can also contain variables from neighboring recordings, so the loader must select the variable that matches the file identifier rather than simply taking the first \texttt{DE\_time} array.

The reproduced experiment in this section uses a deliberately conservative file-level holdout. Normal and 0.007-inch inner-race-fault recordings at 0, 1, and 2 hp are used for training, while the 3 hp normal and fault recordings are held out completely for testing. No window from the held-out recordings appears in model fitting. This design asks whether the learned diagnostic transfers across operating load rather than whether it can recognize another window from a recording it has already seen.

\textbf{Objective:} The objective is to test whether a compact, physically interpretable vibration representation can distinguish normal from seeded inner-race bearing faults under an operating-load shift without recording-level leakage.

\textbf{Problem:} The task is binary fault diagnosis from drive-end vibration: distinguish a normal bearing from a bearing with a seeded 0.007-inch inner-race fault. The engineering objective is not to estimate field failure prevalence, but to test whether simple window-level vibration features remain discriminative under an unseen motor load.

\textbf{Dataset:} The experiment uses CWRU normal files 97--100 and inner-race-fault files 105--108. The four normal files correspond to 0--3 hp motor loads, as do the four 0.007-inch inner-race-fault files. Normal recordings are resampled to 12 kHz so that all signals share the analysis sampling rate used for the 12-kHz drive-end fault subset. The loader explicitly selects \texttt{X097\_DE\_time} through \texttt{X100\_DE\_time} for the normal files and \texttt{X105\_DE\_time} through \texttt{X108\_DE\_time} for the fault files.

\textbf{Model:} Two conventional baselines are evaluated: standardized logistic regression and a random forest. The purpose is not to identify a state-of-the-art bearing-diagnostics architecture, but to determine whether a small set of physically interpretable summary features already separates the two conditions under the file-level load holdout.

\textbf{Method:} Each recording is divided into non-overlapping 2048-sample windows. For each window, the feature vector contains RMS amplitude, crest factor, kurtosis, spectral centroid, and the fraction of spectral energy above 2 kHz. Listing~\ref{lst:ch1-bearing-features} shows the corresponding windowing and spectral-feature computation. The feature extraction is performed only after sampling-rate alignment. Training uses six entire recordings: normal and fault at 0, 1, and 2 hp. Testing uses only the two unseen 3 hp recordings.

\textbf{Metrics:} The principal metrics are held-out balanced accuracy and ROC--AUC on the completely unseen 3 hp recording pair, supported by time-domain, spectral, and feature-space diagnostics that explain the observed class separation.  Figure~\ref{fig:ch1-predictive-maintenance-cwru} provides the real-data diagnostic. Panel (a) compares short time-domain segments from the held-out normal and fault recordings. Panel (b) compares their normalized spectra up to 4 kHz. Panel (c) shows the held-out-window geometry in RMS--kurtosis space, where the two conditions are visibly separated. Table~\ref{tab:ch1-predictive-maintenance-benchmark} reports classifier performance on the same held-out recording pair.

\textbf{Results:} Both classifiers achieve balanced accuracy and ROC--AUC of 1.000 on the held-out 3 hp recording pair. Figure~\ref{fig:ch1-predictive-maintenance-cwru} explains why: the seeded inner-race-fault recording differs strongly from the normal baseline in amplitude distribution, high-frequency content, RMS, and kurtosis even after the operating load changes. In this experiment, the representation does more of the discriminative work than the classifier family.

\textbf{Error Analysis:} There are no classification errors in the held-out pair, so the scientifically important question is not which samples were misclassified but why the benchmark is so separable. The fault type and size are fixed, the apparatus is controlled, and each class is represented by one recording at the held-out load. Error analysis must therefore shift from sample-level mistakes to stress testing the protocol: different fault types, smaller fault sizes, sensor locations, noise levels, and machines are the conditions most likely to reduce the observed separation.

\textbf{Limitations:} This experiment is a controlled diagnostic demonstration, not a fleet-reliability benchmark. It distinguishes only normal bearings from one seeded inner-race fault size on one test apparatus. The held-out-load design prevents recording leakage, but it does not test transfer across machines, bearing geometries, acquisition systems, or naturally developing faults. The perfect classifier scores are therefore evidence that this particular task is easy under the chosen representation, not evidence of universally reliable predictive maintenance.

\textbf{Deployment / Engineering Implications:} A production predictive-maintenance system should preserve the discipline used here while substantially broadening the validation domain. Windows from the same asset or recording must remain grouped, preprocessing must be learned or fixed without using future data, and performance must be stratified by operating condition and asset identity. The central Foundations lesson is that trustworthy performance begins with the split and the representation: a sophisticated classifier cannot compensate for leakage, and a perfect score on a narrow controlled benchmark does not eliminate the need for domain-shift validation.
